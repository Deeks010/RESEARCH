# Kenesis Vision: validation and research playbook

Prepared 15 September 2026. These are proposed experiments and decision rules, not established market facts or completed customer research.

## The decision to make

Identify one factory problem that existing cameras can observe, a named manager can act on, and a budget owner will pay repeatedly to solve. Prove that Kenesis can deliver it across customers without rebuilding the product each time.

The founder confirms unpaid pilots and no paid traction. India is the practical first market. Requested features are workstation absence, chitchatting, SOP validation, restricted-area entry, a CCTV-data chatbot and fall detection. There are no paid-customer successes to assume. Outcomes, deployment costs and willingness to pay remain the most valuable missing inputs.

## First-customer evidence sheet

Record one row per site. Use anonymized names in shared research.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Factory and process | Industry, production process, geography, shifts, scale |
| Paid problem | What the buyer asked to fix; why it mattered then |
| Buyer | Budget owner, daily user, IT approver, procurement steps |

| Camera suitability | Cameras surveyed, compatible streams, useful views, additions required |
| Deployment effort | Staff hours, site visits, network work, computing equipment, training |
| Product reuse | Configuration versus new engineering; changes needed at the next site |
| Detection results | Correct alerts, false alerts, missed events, usable monitoring hours |
| Response | Who acted; response time; interventions completed |
| Outcome | Before/after result, comparable output/exposure, other changes occurring |

## Customer discovery: proposed 15 interviews

Start with five pilot or closely related factories, if accessible. Add five unfamiliar factories in the same process. Add five buyers who chose another vendor or rejected vision software. This is a target sample, not an assumption that five pilot customers exist. It is purposive learning, not a statistically representative survey.

1. What happened the last time this problem cost you money or interrupted work?
2. Show the log or report you used to estimate the loss. What part is avoidable?
3. How do you handle it today? What does that cost, including staff time?
4. Which suppliers have you actually evaluated? Why did you buy or decline?
5. Which camera sees the event? Can a person reliably identify it from ordinary footage?
6. Who responds to an alert? What can they change during the shift?
7. Who pays? Which budget and approval steps would apply?
8. What data restrictions or employee concerns could block adoption?
9. What result would justify a paid pilot and then an annual subscription?
10. What would make you stop using it after three months?

Ask for a concrete paid pilot after establishing the problem. Compliments, survey interest and nonbinding letters do not establish willingness to pay. Obtain permission before viewing or retaining customer footage.

## One comparison brief for vendors

Prepare this brief before requesting quotes. No messages or demo requests have been sent as part of this research.

Use the same factory, camera list, footage sample, event definitions, retention requirements and integration requirements for each quote. Ask for separate prices for the pilot, first year, renewal, extra cameras, local computing, cloud processing, support and integrations. Identify taxes and regional availability. Compare the same scope; a PPE starter kit and an enterprise annual platform are not equivalent.

Require answers to these five groups:

| Group | Questions |
|---|---|
| Camera/compute | Supported streams and resolutions? Existing recorder retained? Who provides computing? What happens offline? |
| Detection | Which exact events? Site calibration? False alerts per camera-hour? Missed-event test? Limits in poor views? |
| Action | Live response versus retrospective review? Assignment and closure? Export into current plant tools? |
| Commercial | All-in cost? Minimum term? Renewal increase? Support exclusions? Termination and data export? |
| Evidence | Reference from the same industry? Measured baseline? Sample size? Outcome duration? Any independent verification? |

## Pilot design

**Scope:** One repeated process, one buyer, one or two event types, three to five useful camera views. The camera count is a proposed starting scope, not a technical guarantee.

**Weeks 1–2: baseline and blind evaluation.** Inspect footage across shifts, lighting conditions and product variants. Define the event before tuning. Reserve footage from another day or site for final evaluation. Have a trained reviewer label events independently. Resolve disputed labels. Sample non-alert periods too; alert review alone cannot reveal missed events.

**Weeks 3–4: silent monitoring.** Record correct alerts, false alerts, missed events, availability and delay. Merge repeated alerts for the same event. Keep event denominators and monitoring exposure comparable. A camera moved out of position should generate a coverage warning, not silently lower incident counts.

**Weeks 5–6: operational test.** Assign an owner to each actionable event. Track intervention, time to response, and a business outcome. Where practical compare a matched shift or staggered rollout. Document staffing, workload, product mix and other changes. This is usually an observational pilot; avoid causal guarantees.

For rare safety injuries, a six-week pilot cannot credibly establish reduced injury rates. Measure independently checked leading indicators such as exposure-normalized near misses and corrective-action closure. Keep existing safety controls in place.

## Proposed gates before wider selling

These are management thresholds to agree with each buyer, not industry standards.

| Gate | Proposed starting threshold | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence | Buyer agrees baseline and event definition before launch | Avoid changing success criteria after results |
| Usability | At least 90% of alerts correct; false-alert workload acceptable to the responder | Accuracy alone hides operational burden |
| Coverage | Missed-event rate measured on independently reviewed footage; threshold set by event severity | Frequent correct alerts do not prove complete detection |
| Transfer | Same workflow works at three unrelated sites; no new bespoke model at every site | Tests whether this is a repeatable product |
| Adoption | Named owner acts on at least 80% of actionable alerts within an agreed window | Detection without intervention has limited value |
| Economics | Buyer-verified annual benefit at least three times total annual cost; target payback under 12 months | Leaves room for imperfect benefit realization |
| Commercial | At least two of three pilots convert at a price covering delivery and support | Tests willingness to pay |

Reject or redesign an offer when useful camera coverage is low, the observable event does not explain the loss, operators cannot act, or configuration costs erase the subscription margin. Safety-critical applications require their own risk and performance assessment; the generic thresholds above are insufficient for protective control systems.

## Revenue and cost model

Use the following relationships. All quantities must come from Kenesis records or explicit assumptions.

Annual recurring revenue = active paying sites × annual recurring fee per site.

Recurring gross profit = recurring revenue − recurring computing, storage, support and directly attributable service costs.

Contribution after acquisition = recurring gross profit − customer acquisition and unrecovered onboarding costs. Keep one-time setup revenue and costs separately visible. Account for partner-provided equipment in the buyer's total cost even if Kenesis does not sell it.

For productivity: annual benefit = recovered productive hours × incremental contribution per productive hour × fraction actually usable. Extra theoretical capacity has limited financial value when orders, labor or another machine constrain output. Avoid counting the same recovered output as both downtime savings and increased revenue.

For safety: do not convert every near miss into an avoided injury. Report verified risk changes separately from hypothetical injury-cost scenarios.

## Research operating method

The research was split into global competitors, regional competitors and market evidence. The lead researcher separately examined production-focused alternatives and checked major pricing and positioning claims. Results are saved outside chat so future work can build on the record.

Maintain a claim register with: company, exact claim, use case, geography, source URL, publisher, publication date when available, date checked, evidence type, confidence and unresolved question. Distinguish company marketing, named vendor-hosted customer stories, buyer-authored reports, regulator data and our own inference.

On refresh, inspect official product, pricing, deployment, customer and legal pages. Recheck the top five direct rivals monthly during active sales. Recheck the wider set quarterly. These are suggested manual cadences; no scheduled monitoring has been installed.

Use scripts for arithmetic, duplicate URLs and missing citations. Do not let scraped summaries decide whether a vendor lacks a feature. Primary pages can also be stale or promotional. Use dated customer documents and live quote responses for consequential claims.

## Known research limits and remedies

| Limit | Remedy |
|---|---|
| Public pages hide pricing and deal terms | Comparable written quotes |
| Vendor cases favor successes | Buyer references, lost-deal interviews and independent pilot labels |
| Broad searches miss small regional rivals | Ask every buyer who else quoted and who installed their CCTV |
| Feature descriptions conceal performance | Shared footage and event definitions |
| Internet research cannot prove Kenesis advantage | Paid pilots, unit economics and renewal evidence |
| Long research can lose context | Persist findings, evidence links and unresolved decisions in this research pack |

The output is a sourced market assessment and a practical validation plan. It does not claim exhaustive coverage of every competitor, completed primary interviews, live product testing or proven product–market fit.
